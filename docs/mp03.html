Written Analysis

About 2,062,223 Korean Americans live across the United States out of 334,922,499 people total. While Korean Americans live in many states, they aren’t spread evenly across the map. Larger groups tend to show up in major metro areas, where jobs, family ties, and established communities are often discussed as key influences on settlement. Looking at where people live helps show how these communities take shape and why certain places hold larger populations over time.

Based on 2024 ACS 5 year estimates, Los Angeles has the largest Korean American population at 343,006 people (16.63% of the national total), followed by New York–Newark–Jersey City at 225,542 (10.94%) and Washington, DC at 108,241 (5.25%). Together, Los Angeles and New York make up 27.57% of Korean Americans nationwide. Still, raw counts tell a different story than local shares. Urban Honolulu has fewer Korean Americans (42,534) than Los Angeles, yet they make up 4.25% of Honolulu’s total population compared to 2.64% in Los Angeles.

Local maps show how communities spread out within a region rather than sitting in just one spot. Across Los Angeles County, approximate place level estimates created by assigning each census tract to its largest overlapping Census Place put the City of Los Angeles highest in headcount at 113,594 residents (2.94% of the city), while suburban places often show higher local shares, such as Cerritos at 11.79% (5,643 people), La Mirada at 11.28% (5,323), and Torrance at 8.61% (12,243). In New York City, neighborhood data points to strong clustering in Queens led by Murray Hill Broadway Flushing with about 7,944 Korean American residents and the NYC map shows that certain census tracts in northeastern Queens, especially around Flushing, reach much higher Korean American population percentages than most other parts of the city.

Historical context on Korean immigrants distinct from the Census estimates for the overall Korean American population, which includes U.S. born residents helps explain why these hubs grew. Research on Korean immigrants notes that the 1965 immigration law led to larger waves of Korean arrivals, with many settling in places like California and New York alongside family connections and major metro opportunities (Esterline & Batalova, 2022). Over time, local churches and community institutions helped support families and newcomers across urban centers and suburbs (Zhou & Kim, 2007). While census numbers alone can’t prove why individuals choose specific places, pairing them with historical research gives a fuller picture of how migration history and community networks align with today’s geographic patterns.
